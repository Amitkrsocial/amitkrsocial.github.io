# How to Get Your First 10 Users Without Paid Ads

Launching a website, app, or software product is exciting. Getting people to use it is often much harder.

You can spend weeks building features, improving the design, and preparing a launch announcement, only to discover that very few people sign up. The problem is not always the product. Often, potential users simply do not know it exists, do not understand its value, or have not yet found a reason to try it.

The good news is that you do not need a large marketing budget to find your first users. You need a clear audience, a problem worth solving, a straightforward way to reach potential customers, and a process for learning from their responses.

This guide explains how to find your first 10 users without paid advertising, using practical methods that independent developers, founders, bloggers, and small businesses can apply.

## 1. Define Exactly Who Your Product Is For

Before promoting your product, identify the people most likely to need it.

A product designed for everyone is difficult to explain and even harder to market. A product designed for a specific audience gives you a clearer message and helps you identify where potential users spend their time.

Start by answering these questions:

- Who experiences the problem your product solves?
- How do they currently deal with it?
- What makes their existing solution inconvenient?
- Where do they look for advice or alternatives?
- What would convince them to try something new?

For example, instead of targeting anyone interested in writing, a creative writing app might focus on people who regularly write quotations, publish poetry, or create shareable text-based content.

Similarly, a product discovery platform might focus on independent founders who want to introduce their products to early adopters.

Write a simple audience statement:

"My product helps [specific audience] solve [specific problem] by [clear benefit]."

Use this sentence to guide your outreach, content, and landing-page copy.

## 2. Talk to Potential Users Before Promoting Your Product

One of the easiest mistakes to make is promoting a product before understanding whether people actually need it.

Talk to a few potential users first. Ask how they currently solve the problem, what frustrates them, and which alternatives they have already tried.

Avoid questions that encourage polite answers, such as "Do you like my idea?" Instead, ask about real behavior:

- When did you last experience this problem?
- What did you do to solve it?
- How much time or effort did it take?
- What was missing from the existing solution?
- Have you paid for a tool or service to address it?

Actual behavior is more informative than hypothetical enthusiasm.

You do not need hundreds of interviews. Begin with five to ten relevant conversations and look for recurring problems. If people describe the same frustration independently, you have a useful starting point for your positioning.

For a deeper approach to finding and interviewing early users, see [this practical guide to getting your first 10 users](https://prodlaunchpad.com/blog/how-to-get-your-first-10-users-a-practical-2-week-plan-2).

## 3. Create a Simple Landing Page

Your landing page should help visitors understand three things quickly: what the product does, who it is for, and what they should do next.

You do not need a complicated website. A focused page can include:

1. A headline describing the main benefit.
2. A short explanation of the problem.
3. A few features connected to user needs.
4. Screenshots or a short product demonstration.
5. Testimonials or evidence, if you have genuine ones.
6. One clear call to action.

Avoid filling the page with technical details that only the development team understands. Explain the outcome rather than listing features without context.

For example, "Create and share visually appealing quotations" describes an outcome more clearly than "Advanced typography and image rendering engine."

Make sure the page works on mobile devices, loads reliably, and has a working signup or download link.

If you are still building the product, use a waitlist or early-access form. Explain what people can expect and how you will contact them.

## 4. Find Users Where They Already Spend Time

You do not need to build an audience from scratch before reaching potential customers. Begin with communities and platforms where people already discuss the problem you solve.

Depending on your audience, useful places may include:

- Reddit communities related to your product category.
- LinkedIn groups and professional discussions.
- Relevant Discord or Slack communities.
- Niche forums and specialist websites.
- Industry newsletters and creator communities.
- Existing professional and personal networks.

Spend time understanding each community before sharing a link. Read its rules, answer questions, and contribute information that is useful even when nobody visits your website.

For example, a founder building a productivity app might answer questions about a common workflow problem and explain a practical solution. If the product is genuinely relevant, mentioning it briefly may help, provided the community permits promotion.

Do not copy the same promotional message into dozens of groups. That approach can appear spammy and may get your accounts or posts restricted.

**The goal is to become useful in the places where your potential users already gather.**

## 5. Use Helpful Content to Attract the Right People

Content marketing can help you reach potential users before they know your product exists.

Instead of writing only about your product, create resources around the problems your audience is trying to solve. These might include tutorials, comparisons, checklists, original research, practical examples, or answers to frequently asked questions.

A useful content workflow starts with a clear reader problem, reliable research, and a structured explanation. My guide on [building a content workflow that turns research into useful articles](/blog/content-workflow-for-bloggers/) explains how to plan, research, write, and improve content systematically.

Search optimization can help these resources reach people actively looking for answers. For additional context, read [Google's official guide to creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

For websites exploring visibility in AI-powered search, [this guide to Generative Engine Optimization](https://buddymantra.com/what-is-generative-engine-optimization/) offers another perspective on making content useful and understandable across modern search experiences.

Remember that organic search takes time. A new article may not attract meaningful traffic immediately, so combine long-term content creation with direct conversations and community participation.

## 6. Make Personal Outreach Part of Your Routine

When your product is new, direct outreach can be more effective than waiting for visitors to arrive.

Identify 30 people who fit your target audience. You do not need to contact all of them immediately. Begin with a small group and personalize each message.

A useful outreach message is short, specific, and respectful.

For example:

> Hi [Name], I noticed your work on [relevant project or topic]. I'm building a tool designed to help [audience] with [specific problem]. I'm speaking with people who experience this problem and would value your perspective. Would you be open to sharing how you currently handle it?

If the person expresses interest, explain the product and offer an easy next step.

Avoid pretending to know someone personally, sending repeated unsolicited messages, or making claims you cannot support. Respect a refusal and follow the rules of the platform you use.

Keep a simple spreadsheet with the person's name, why they are relevant, the date contacted, their response, and any follow-up needed.

This makes outreach manageable without requiring an expensive customer relationship management system.

## 7. Offer a Small Beta Experience

Some people are interested in a product but are not ready to commit to using it regularly. A limited beta experience can help them try the product while giving you valuable feedback.

Choose a small group of people who actually experience the problem you are solving. Give each person a specific task instead of simply asking them to explore the product.

Afterward, ask:

- What was easy to understand?
- Where did you get stuck?
- What did you expect the product to do?
- Would you use it again?
- What would prevent you from recommending it?

Watch what people do, not only what they say. Someone may praise a product but never return to it. Repeated use and meaningful feedback are stronger signals of value.

Record the most common problems and fix the biggest obstacles first. You do not need to implement every feature request. Look for the underlying need shared by several users.

For a more detailed testing process, see [how to find beta users for a startup](https://prodlaunchpad.com/blog/how-to-find-beta-users-for-startup).

## 8. Turn Your First Users Into a Learning Loop

Getting 10 signups is a useful milestone, but the more important question is whether those people benefit from the product.

Track a few simple indicators:

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Qualified visitors | Whether you are reaching the right audience |
| Signups | Whether the offer motivates people to try the product |
| Activation | Whether users complete the main action |
| Returning users | Whether the product provides ongoing value |
| Feedback | What needs to improve |
| Referrals | Whether users are willing to recommend it |

You do not need sophisticated analytics to begin. A spreadsheet and a basic analytics tool may be enough.

If people visit but do not sign up, review your landing page and offer. If they sign up but never complete the main action, examine onboarding. If they use the product once and disappear, investigate whether it solves a recurring problem.

This is more productive than assuming that every problem can be solved by increasing traffic.

## A Practical Two-Week Plan to Find Your First 10 Users

Use this schedule as a starting point, not a guarantee of results.

### Days 1–2: Define the audience

- Write your one-sentence product description.
- Identify the problem you solve.
- List 30 potential users.
- Review competing products and existing alternatives.

### Days 3–5: Start conversations

- Contact five relevant people each day.
- Ask about their existing workflow.
- Record recurring frustrations.
- Refine your positioning based on what you learn.

### Days 6–7: Improve the product page

- Make the headline more specific.
- Clarify the primary benefit.
- Test the signup or download process.
- Publish one useful resource related to the problem.

### Days 8–10: Invite early users

- Invite interested people to try the product.
- Help them complete the main task.
- Record where they struggle.
- Fix the most important usability issues.

### Days 11–14: Follow up and learn

- Ask early users about their experience.
- Check whether they return.
- Improve onboarding or messaging.
- Ask satisfied users whether they know someone else with the same problem.

You might acquire 10 users during this period, or it might take longer. The value of the process is that every conversation and test teaches you something about your audience.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Chasing followers instead of users.** A large social following does not necessarily mean people need your product.

**Promoting before understanding the problem.** Your messaging will be weaker if it is based on assumptions rather than real conversations.

**Trying every marketing channel at once.** Start with one or two places where your target audience is active.

**Building features before collecting feedback.** More functionality does not automatically make a product more useful.

**Expecting SEO to work overnight.** Organic discovery can take time, particularly for a new domain or website.

**Measuring only signups.** Returning users and successful completion of the main task provide better evidence of product value.

## Conclusion

Finding your first 10 users without paid advertising is primarily a process of learning and direct engagement.

Define a specific audience, talk to people who experience the problem, make your offer clear, and reach potential users in places where they already spend time. Use helpful content to support discovery, offer a focused beta experience, and improve the product based on actual behavior.

Do not wait for a perfect launch or a large audience. Start with five relevant conversations, learn what people need, and build from there.

Your first 10 users are not simply a number to reach. They can help you understand who benefits from your product, why they choose it, and what you need to improve before trying to reach the next 100.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you get your first 10 users without advertising?

Yes. Direct outreach, relevant online communities, referrals, useful content, and beta testing can help you find early users without paid advertising. Results depend on the product, audience, and quality of execution.

### How many people should you contact?

Start with around 30 carefully selected prospects. Contact a manageable number each day and personalize your messages. Focus on learning from the responses rather than sending as many messages as possible.

### Should your first users get free access?

Free access can lower the barrier to trying a new product, particularly during beta testing. However, whether you should charge depends on your business model and the value being delivered. Genuine willingness to pay is an important signal for commercial products.

### How long does it take to get the first 10 users?

There is no universal timeline. A focused outreach effort may generate early users within a couple of weeks, but products with complex onboarding or narrow audiences may take longer. Use the process to improve your understanding of the market even when growth is slower than expected.

### What should you do after getting 10 users?

Find out which users receive the most value, why they chose the product, and whether they return. Fix the biggest obstacles, refine your messaging, and ask satisfied users for introductions. Use what you learn to make the next stage of acquisition more repeatable.
